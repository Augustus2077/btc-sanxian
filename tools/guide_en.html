0 · The one-minute version

The three-line convergence (三线合一) is a chart by Murphy (@Murphychen888): it stacks Bitcoin’s MVRV curve after each halving, aligned by “days since the halving”. The red line is this cycle (2024-04-20 halving), green is the 2020 cycle and blue the 2016 cycle. Where the three lines stick together is a “convergence”.

It rests on just two foundations. First, MVRV = price ÷ the network’s average cost basis: it measures how many times over the average holder is in profit, a valuation gauge that doesn’t depend on the price level. Second, the halving is the only clock hard-coded into Bitcoin. Putting “valuation” on the “clock” shows which historical stage this cycle has reached, and whether it is more or less expensive than at the same point in history.

The most important sentence (tested on the data): a convergence is not a “buy signal” in itself but a “synchronization”. When the three lines stick together, this cycle’s valuation matches history at the same point, and from there it most likely follows the historical lines: if they go up next, synchronization is bullish; if they go down, it is bearish. Backtesting every quantified convergence (13 through 2026-10, see 3.3), the red line’s direction 180 days later matched the average direction of the two historical lines every time: 9 went up, all in a bottoming or recovery phase; 4 went down, all on the way from a cycle top to the bear-market bottom, one of them this cycle’s 2025-12-30 to 2026-01-07.

How to read the live box above: the red line sitting between green and blue is normal; running to the top or the bottom is a “deviation”. There are only three ways for the red line to rejoin the historical lines: price falls; time lets the historical lines rise to meet it; or this cycle decouples from history (3.4, 5.3). The chart can’t tell you which, but it can tell you which dates to watch: with four timing analogs it moves the previous three cycles’ bottoms, and the “last straw” before each, onto this cycle’s calendar (tables 5 and 7). With only three samples, this is a sense of rhythm, not a forecast.

Common questions (where we are in the cycle today, whether “a black swan always arrives on schedule” holds up, and how long recovery takes after a bear market) are answered in section 8.

1 · Foundation one: what MVRV actually measures

1.1 Realized price RP: the network’s average “last-moved price”

Bitcoin’s ledger doesn’t record “account balances”; it records individual chunks of coins called UTXOs (unspent transaction outputs). The moment each UTXO last moved on chain is known, and the market price at that moment is its “realized price”.

Realized Cap = Σ (each UTXO’s amount × the price when it last moved)
Realized Price RP = Realized Cap ÷ circulating supply
MVRV (Market Value to Realized Value) = market cap ÷ realized cap = price ÷ RP

So RP can be read as “the network’s average cost basis”, or more precisely “the average price at which every coin last changed hands”. MVRV is “how many times that average cost the current price is”.

1.2 A small example: when RP rises and when it falls

Suppose the whole network has just 4 coins (purely illustrative):

HolderAmountPrice when it last moved
A1 BTC$20,000
B1 BTC$60,000
C1 BTC$100,000
D (mined early, never moved)1 BTC$1,000

RP = (20,000 + 60,000 + 100,000 + 1,000) ÷ 4 = $45,250. At a price of $84,000, MVRV = 84,000 ÷ 45,250 = 1.86.

Case A · capitulation. Price drops to $50,000 and C can’t take it any more, selling the coin bought at $100,000 to E for $50,000. That coin’s “last-moved price” goes from 100,000 to 50,000, and RP drops from $45,250 to $32,750 (−28%). At the same $50,000 price, MVRV goes from 1.10 before the sale to 1.53 after it.

Case B · profit-taking. Price is still $84,000 and A sells the coin bought at $20,000 to F. Its last-moved price becomes 84,000, RP rises to $61,250, and MVRV drops from 1.86 to 1.37 without the price moving at all.

These two cases run through the whole guide. First, RP only changes when coins move: in a bear market, trapped holders selling at a loss pull RP down, a process this page calls the “cost reset”; in a bull market, old coins sold at a profit push RP up. Second, that is why the MVRV peak tends to come before the price peak: heavy turnover late in a bull market lifts RP, so the same price means a lower MVRV. The 2020 cycle shows it: MVRV peaked at 3.96 on 2021-02-21, when RP was about $14.5k; when price peaked at $69,000 on 2021-11-10, RP had risen to $23.8k and MVRV was only 2.72.

A correction while we’re here: in his 2025-02-04 post Murphy described “MVRV above 3” as “an average of 300% unrealized profit”. Strictly, MVRV = 3 means market cap is 3× the cost basis, i.e. an unrealized gain of 200% of cost. A closely related metric is NUPL (Net Unrealized Profit/Loss) = 1 − 1/MVRV: MVRV 3 corresponds to NUPL 0.67, and MVRV 1 to 0.

1.4 Why compare cycles with MVRV rather than price

The four bear-market bottoms were at $152, $3,122, $15,479 and, so far this cycle, $57,735: hundreds of times apart. The cycles’ MVRV lows were 0.56, 0.69, 0.75 and, so far this cycle, 1.10: all on the same scale. Price is magnitude; MVRV is “how holders are doing”. Greed and fear repeat, price levels don’t, so you can only stack MVRV, not price. Switch the main chart’s lower panel to “Price” and you can see that the four price lines aren’t even in the same order of magnitude.

Live Each cycle’s MVRV peak (▲) and low (▼); the colors match the cycles. Peaks get lower every cycle and lows get higher: the swings are converging. Every later observation that “this cycle doesn’t look like history” traces back to this.

1.5 A structural fact: MVRV bunches up at bottoms and spreads out at tops

MVRV has a “soft floor” at the bottom, roughly between 0.6 and 1.0. On one hand, by the bottom almost everyone willing to sell at a loss has already sold; on the other, capitulation itself pulls RP down (case A in 1.2), which pushes MVRV back up, a kind of self-repair. There is no ceiling at the top, though: it depends on how much new money arrives and how manic sentiment gets.

The result: MVRV curves from different cycles naturally tend to come together at bear-market bottoms and drift apart at bull-market tops. So a “three-line convergence at the bottom” is half structural inevitability and only half pattern. That doesn’t refute it; it explains why convergences at the bottom are more reliable than convergences elsewhere.

2 · Foundation two: why align by the halving

2.1 The halving is Bitcoin’s only hard-coded clock

Every 210,000 blocks, the miners’ block subsidy halves. Block heights are fixed, so the halving is the only on-chain event that is known in advance and identical in every cycle. The block times below were checked against mempool.space.

HalvingBlock heightBlock time (UTC)Block subsidyNew supply per day (approx.)
1st210,0002012-11-28 15:24:3850 → 25 BTC7,200 → 3,600
2nd420,0002016-07-09 16:46:1325 → 12.53,600 → 1,800
3rd630,0002020-05-11 19:23:4312.5 → 6.251,800 → 900
4th840,0002024-04-20 00:09:276.25 → 3.125900 → 450

The causal chain behind “halvings drive the cycle” goes: the new coins miners must sell each day are halved → selling pressure falls → with demand unchanged, price is pushed up → the rise attracts new money and sentiment overheats → a top → a bear market clears it out. Whether that chain is right is disputed (see section 7), but the regularity of the timing is hard data.

2.2 D numbers and “month k”

This page uses D for “days after the halving”, with the halving day as D0. Note that “month k” counts from 1: Murphy’s “months 29–33” means 28 to 33 months have elapsed, i.e. roughly D852 to D1004, not a window starting at D883. That one-month difference decides where the window is drawn; the blue time windows on this page use this definition.

3 · Three-line convergence: what Murphy is actually saying

3.1 How the chart is drawn

Cut MVRV history into four segments at the halving dates, start each one at D0 and line up the same D vertically. Murphy’s colors this cycle (from 2025): red = this cycle (2024), green = the 2020 cycle, blue = the 2016 cycle. His 2022–2023 charts used different colors: red = the 2020 cycle, green = 2012, blue = 2016. This page’s “Last cycle” view uses those older colors so you can compare directly with his original charts; the “Murphy’s chart style” button on the first tab switches to the look of his current chart in one click (only red, green and blue, no lower panel or markers, x-axis from 2024-05). The x-axis shows the calendar of the selected cycle; hover to see the real date of each point on a historical line.

3.2 His rules (as summarized by this page)

  1. Convergence = the best buy zone. Where the three lines stick together has historically been the best place to buy. In the last cycle he marked two: the major bottom in November–December 2022 ($16k–$18k) and the second bottoming in September–October 2023 ($26k–$28k).
  2. Timing rules. In months 11–12 after the halving, all three lines’ MVRV rises above 3; in months 29–33 they all fall below 1; then comes a rebound of 5–6 months.
  3. Blue and green in sync. When the blue and green historical lines move in the same direction, the red line most likely follows.
  4. Deviations revert. The red line spends most of its time between blue and green; after running to the top or the bottom (a “deviation”), it tends to come back to the middle.
  5. Scaled price. A historical line’s MVRV × the current RP gives “the price if this cycle reached the same valuation”. RP changes daily, which is why he always adds “(dynamic)” to such prices.
  6. Positioning. In his own words, 99% of his analysis rests on data with an underlying logic (holder-age cohorts, turnover cost, financial pressure, sentiment); the three-line convergence alone is a “metaphysical indicator”, to be used only when everything else agrees.

3.3 The right reading after testing it on data: a “synchronization” signal, not a “buy signal” Live

“Sticking together” has to be quantified before it can be tested. This page defines: spread = (max − min of the three lines’ MVRV) ÷ their average; a spread below 10% lasting at least 5 days (with gaps of up to 3 days merged into one stretch) counts as one “convergence”. The definition is this page’s; Murphy judges by eye. But 10% exactly reproduces the two he marked by hand (2022-11-08 to 12-07 and 2023-09-20 to 10-04), so the definition isn’t off.

Scanning both perspectives that can be backtested (the 2020 cycle against 2016 / 2012, and this cycle against 2020 / 2016) gives the convergence periods below, and what followed each one:

Each dot is one convergence: the x-axis is the D when it ended, the y-axis the red line’s price change over the next 180 days. ▲ up, ▼ down; green dots are the backtest with the 2020 cycle as the red line, red dots this cycle. The light background marks the historical “top → bottom” declines.

The pattern is very clean (through 2026-10): the red line’s direction 180 days later matched the average direction of the two historical lines every single time. The good ones were all in bottom zones (2022-11) or recoveries (2020-05, 2023-07 to 12, 2024-08 to 10); the bad ones all happened on the way down from a top to the bottom (twice in 2022-01, 2022-05, and this cycle’s 2025-12-30 to 2026-01-07), when the two historical lines were themselves heading down.

Bluntly: remembering only “convergence = buy signal” will hurt you. The convergence from 2025-12-30 to 2026-01-07 had the red line at $87.5k–$93.9k; over the next 180 days price fell 30%, with a maximum drawdown of 37%. The right question is always: “It converged; so where do the historical lines go next?”

The threshold is just a dial. From 6% to 12%, the 2022-01, 2022-11, 2023-07 to 08 and 2023-09 to 10 convergences always show up (2022-01 being the bad kind), while a few marginal ones come and go. Drag the threshold slider on the first tab to see for yourself.

4.4 The key-date tables, table by table and column by column

Table 1 · Key dates of each cycle. The top is given two ways: “intraday high” comes from Bitstamp’s daily high and “highest close” from CoinMetrics’ UTC close; they can be a day or two apart. The MVRV peak usually comes before the price peak, for the reason given in 1.2. The bottom likewise has three versions: intraday low, lowest close and MVRV low. The 2020 cycle is the textbook case: the MVRV low came on 2022-11-09 (during the FTX blow-up, 0.754, close $15,758), the intraday price low on 2022-11-21 ($15,479), yet that day’s MVRV was higher, at 0.778. The reason: in those two weeks panic sellers capitulated en masse, pulling RP from about $20.9k to $20.3k, a cost reset, so the same price meant a higher MVRV. Path B · time absorbs it.

7 · Limits, and the claim that “the big cycle isn’t affected by outside events”

7.1 Too few samples

There are only three complete cycles, and the 2012 market was so small that strictly it’s two and a half. “Every cycle…” is statistically just 3 data points. Worse is the multiple-comparisons problem: the more metrics, windows and thresholds you look at, the more likely you are to hit a coincidental match. Both timing rules held in the previous three cycles and both failed in this one, which is a warning.

7.2 The cycle is decaying

Peak MVRV, maximum drawdown, bull-market gains (previous bottom to this cycle’s top: 129× → 22× → 8.2×) and the depth of the cost reset are all converging. The more mature and the larger an asset, the smaller its swings; that is the norm, not a quirk of this cycle.

7.3 On “the big cycle isn’t affected by outside events”

The pattern many people see is real: the timing of the tops (D526, D548, D534 after the halving) is almost to the day. But “not affected by outside events” goes too far, and the data has counterexamples. The March 2020 COVID crash pushed the 2016 cycle back below MVRV 1 for six days at the very end of the cycle; in 2022, LUNA (May) and FTX (November) both hit during the bear market and drove the bottom deeper. From price alone you can’t tell whether the cycle “produced” these events or the events “shaped” the cycle (section 8 breaks this down further).

Another school holds that the four-year cycle is really a reflection of the global liquidity cycle (rates, the dollar, credit expansion): the previous three bear markets (2014–15, 2018, 2022) all coincided with a strong dollar or Fed tightening, and the halving merely lines up in time. In this cycle, spot ETFs and institutional holdings changed who owns the coins, which may be one reason volatility has been compressed. Both views have data behind them; there is no consensus yet.

A reading closer to the data may be: so far the rhythm of the big cycle (when) has been very stable, but its amplitude (how high, how deep) is amplified or damped by the outside environment and market structure. Those two are best kept apart.

7.4 Data definitions

MVRV depends on “the price when a coin last moved”, which brings some systematic biases. Lost coins (including early miners’ coins) are counted forever at a very low cost, pulling RP down. Exchanges reshuffling internal wallets and ETF custodians moving cold storage make coins “move” and get repriced at the current price; there was a lot of this in this cycle, which may have lifted RP and depressed MVRV. Data vendors (Glassnode, CoinMetrics) also differ in the details: this page’s data and Looknode (Glassnode data) differ by about 0.1%–0.4%, which doesn’t change any conclusion, but don’t treat 1.93 and 1.94 as two different things.

7.5 Murphy’s own caveats

Every time he discusses the three-line convergence he stresses that it is a “metaphysical indicator”, only to be used when other data with real logic agrees, and he has publicly acknowledged when it failed (in March 2025). The best way to use this chart is as a “time map” and a “valuation thermometer”, not a buy/sell switch.

8 · Common questions: where we are today, “a black swan always arrives on schedule”, and how long until recovery

This section was written in 2026-10 (data through 2026-10-08); tables, charts and numbers marked Live recompute as the data updates, and they take precedence.

This section answers three questions that come up often. First, compared with the same “today” in earlier cycles, where are we now? Second, there is a view that COVID in 2020 and LUNA and FTX in 2022 were no accidents, but that “when the time comes, a black swan always arrives; it only needs an excuse”. By that logic, how far away is this cycle’s black swan? Third, how long until the market recovers after a bear market?

Third, COVID was a different animal: it came from outside, hit 60 days before the 2016 cycle’s halving, was V-shaped, and didn’t break the 2018 bear-market bottom.

8.4 What this view gets right, and what needs correcting

What it gets right: most crypto black swans are endogenous. LUNA, Three Arrows, Celsius and FTX were leverage and holes piled up during the bull market, forced to blow up once prices fell; the decline itself is why they blew up. So their timing is tied to the cycle phase, not random. Think of a sandpile: which grain sets off the avalanche is chance; that the pile reaches a critical state is not.

Four things to correct:

  1. COVID was a different animal. It came from outside, hit 60 days before the halving, was V-shaped and didn’t break the bottom; it doesn’t run on the same clock as FTX. Explaining it as “there is always a drop around the halving” doesn’t hold either: in any 240-day (about 8-month) window, bitcoin has had a ≥20% drawdown about 96% of the time, and a ≥40% drawdown about 57% of the time, so “a drop around every halving” is almost guaranteed and proves nothing. If you insist on watching for it, the next halving is estimated around 2028-04, pointing to late 2027 through May 2028, which has nothing to do with the current window.
  2. The black swan is a flock, not one bird. A more accurate version of “a black swan always arrives on schedule” is: bear markets last about 12–13.5 months, and the final drop always finds an excuse. The regularity is in the length of the bear market, not in the event itself.
  3. Damage equals shock times fragility. When the previous three last straws hit, MVRV was at 0.83–1.13: the average holder was already at or near a loss, and lending platforms and miners were on the edge of bankruptcy. A shock of the same size would leave a much shallower crater now. Outside conditions such as macro tightening don’t decide the timing, but they do change the fragility.
  4. This cycle’s black swan may already have been “delivered” early. Oct 10 was the largest liquidation in crypto history, about $19 billion; Feb 5 saw −13.1% in a day and −24.9% in 7 days, deeper than the FTX week. Jun 30 looks most like the straw that broke the camel’s back: that day Strategy sold bitcoin for the first time, Mt.Gox wallets moved out about $950 million, ETFs had 8 straight days of outflows, and this cycle’s lowest close as of 2026-10 ($58,525) printed, followed the next day by this cycle’s lowest point ($57,735). On the days-after-halving clock, Jun 30 is D801, inside the historical “last straw” range of D768–D911; Jul 1 is D802, inside the historical bottom range of D777–D924. The only mismatch is the distance from the top: 268 days, 95–142 days shorter than in the previous three cycles, because this cycle fell less and fell faster.
